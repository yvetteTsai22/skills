# Chapter 7: Writing — Identity, Measurement, Outlines, Easing Into Drafts, Composing vs. Revising

Sources: https://zettelkasten.de/posts/identity-schedule-serious-writing/ · /posts/how-to-track-writing-progress/ · /posts/count-your-words/ · /posts/manage-writing-ideas/ · /posts/how-i-use-outlines-to-write-any-text/ · /posts/ease-into-writing/ · /posts/write-book-without-even-trying-so-hard/ · /posts/writing-composing-revising/ · /posts/publish-iteratively/ · /posts/henrik-zettelkasten-paper-submission/ (all under https://zettelkasten.de)

## Core Idea
The Zettelkasten's output is text. Writing becomes manageable when you (1) make it part of your **identity** and schedule it, (2) **measure** it with words per day and notes per day, (3) keep writing projects as **outlines or buffer files** in the archive and attach every new note to one, (4) **assemble** drafts from notes instead of starting from a blank page, and (5) separate **composing** (adding) from **revising** (cutting and reshaping). Research and drafting are different modes, and switching between them is expensive.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Make Writing Part of Your Identity** (Christian): lasting change requires an identity shift stated *positively*, as "non-smoker" rather than "quitter". Then: know the outcome, know the path, and ensure you walk it through **appointments with yourself**. "You don't have to *find* time to write." An hour or two daily beats a whole day once in a while.
- **Track progress**: metrics are **notes per day** (research progress), **words per day per project**, goal attainment, and project. Commitments are not tasks. **Don't take the numbers personally.** Set realistic targets from your actual best day, then move high-quality work to your best hours.
- **Count Your Words** (Sascha): productivity *is* words produced. Cycle: measure, experiment, evaluate, adjust. Daily writing carries over to every project, and 500 a day is easier than 3,500 in one day.
- **Indirectional vs. Directional work** (Sascha): *indirectional* means feeding the archive with no project in mind. *Directional* means notes made for a specific text. The bridge: for every new Zettel, search your *Themes and Outlines* (now structure notes) for one that can use it, or create a new outline. Books then emerge "without even trying so hard".
- **Outlines for any text** (Christian): every item is a full sentence, hierarchy gives context, and research notes hang as children so they move together. Reference notes **by ID** (weak coupling) rather than pasting content (strong coupling) until the structure settles. Sascha's variant starts from one reference, adds more, then clusters them under headings.
- **Ease Into Writing: the six-step draft**:
  1. Get an overview of what you know (query the archive and follow trails).
  2. Write a first outline.
  3. Attach notes to outline items.
  4. Fill gaps with targeted research, repeating steps 3–4.
  5. Paste note contents in outline order.
  6. Rewrite.
  - "All first drafts suck, so get over with it."
- **Composing vs. Revising** (Christian): composing is *additive* and revising is *subtractive*. Computer writers mix the two and lose momentum. Compose in your best hours (often mornings) and revise later with fresh distance.
- **Where Writing Ideas Go**: not in a GTD someday/maybe list, because creative work cannot be planned as tasks. Keep project outlines (e.g. files named `- O <name>`) in the archive. Task lists serve only maintenance during drafting, such as research prompts and checking for duplication.
- **Publish Iteratively**: "a book, like large software, is never finished — only released." Release early, collect feedback, revise. Deferring publication builds anxiety.

## Key Concepts
- **Appointment with yourself**: scheduled, protected writing time.
- **Commitment vs. task**: an ongoing endeavour vs. a one-off action.
- **Knowledge Cycle**: research, read, take note, write. Short cycles beat "big-bang" drafting.
- **Buffer file / outline file**: a living project container in the archive.
- **Weak vs. strong coupling**: referencing note IDs vs. pasting text into the draft.
- **Composing / revising**: adding new material vs. improving existing material.
- **Assembly step**: turning linked notes into a rough draft.

## Mental Models
- Think of **writing as integration testing**: integrate notes continuously so you don't discover at the end that the parts don't fit.
- Treat **every note as a vote for several books**. Linking one note to several outlines grows several projects at once (Sascha once ran 1 active, 23 inactive, and 54 candidate projects).
- **Getting the tools out of the way is the goal.** "Do knowledge work and don't bend to the tools of knowledge work."
- **Writing with a Zettelkasten is both easy and hard** (Henrik). Assembly is easy, but a sharp narrative still takes heavy editing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting for inspiration or "finding time"**: life fills every gap.
- **Over-ambitious metrics**: a 5,000-word target when your best is 3,000 means you will abandon tracking.
- **Task-listing a book** ("finish chapter 1, finish chapter 2"): the steps are not actionable and the process cannot be planned.
- **Editing while composing**: switching gears wastes energy and kills flow.
- **Researching and drafting in the same session**: each mode needs time to get into.
- **Pasting everything early**: tangled drafts that you cannot untangle when the structure changes.
- **Expecting the Zettelkasten to write well for you**: it delivers connected ideas, not a polished story.

## Worked Example
Christian's outline for a ReSwift book, kept as an outline file in the archive:

```
* Analyzing ReSwift
    * §201605291256 ReSwift building blocks
* Architecture
    * §201606161929 ReSwift doesn't impose architectural constraints
    * §201606161939 ReSwift's role in a layered architecture
```

An outline-compilation script swaps each `§ID` for that note's text to produce a rough draft. If the project were still only an idea, the outline file would just hold a one-paragraph pitch plus open questions ("Does it scale for complex apps? Does it dictate an architecture?") until notes began to attach.

Henrik's field report shows the payoff and its limits. Notes "left out" of an earlier paper stayed connected in the archive. Exploring his question notes during free time produced a new idea, that two measured properties could predict an optimal shaping factor. The first submission still failed and only a rewritten, sharper narrative was accepted.

## Key Takeaways
1. Schedule writing as appointments, and adopt "writer" as part of your identity.
2. Track notes per day and words per day per project. Set honest targets and don't moralise the numbers.
3. Attach every new note to at least one outline or structure note.
4. Draft by assembling notes in outline order, then rewrite.
5. Compose early, revise later, and never both at once.
6. Release early and iterate.
