# Chapter 6: Reading — Barbell Method, Reading as Searching, Marks, Collect–Process–Write

Sources: https://zettelkasten.de/posts/bibliography-zettelkasten/ · /posts/barbell-method-reading/ · /posts/reading-is-searching/ · /posts/making-proper-marks-in-books/ · /posts/reading-putting-it-all-together/ · /posts/create-zettel-from-reading-notes/ · /posts/reading-web-rss-note-taking/ (all under https://zettelkasten.de)

## Core Idea
Reading feeds the Zettelkasten, but only *processing* turns reading into knowledge. Read quickly to probe a text, mark what matters, then go back and process **only the marked parts** deeply. The quality of the text decides how much time it gets. While reading, *search* for specific kinds of items: theories, models, definitions, arguments, counter-arguments, and facts. Move ideas out of the book into reading notes, cluster them, and write atomic Zettel from the clusters. Keep citations in a reference manager so every note can stand on its own.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Barbell Method of Reading** (Sascha, after Taleb's barbell strategy): cap your downside (time spent on weak books) and leave the upside (insight from great books) unlimited.
  1. **Read** the book swiftly. Don't skip, and mark everything useful, interesting, or inspiring.
  2. **Read again, only the marked parts**, and now take notes, connect them to existing Zettel, and think. A shallow book takes half an hour. *Antifragile* took about twelve deep-work days.
  - Four kinds of passage: (1) useful and hard: process heavily for understanding and use; (2) useful and easy: process heavily for use, and let them inspire writing; (3) not useful but hard: "sharpen your mental teeth", then drop; (4) not useful and easy: ignore.
- **Reading for the Zettelkasten Is Searching** (Sascha): six item types to look for:
  - **Theories**: networks of statements that produce claims about reality.
  - **Models**: representations of reality, such as maps and metaphors.
  - **Definitions**: the building blocks of models and theories.
  - **Arguments**: premise, operator, conclusion. Split multi-premise arguments so each premise links to the conclusion independently.
  - **Counter-arguments**: attack the truth of a premise or the validity of the inference.
  - **Facts**: things you deliberately accept without demanding reasons. Accept them consciously, not by default.
- **Collect → Process → Write** (Christian, GTD-like):
  - **Collect**: unobtrusive marks (a pencil dot `•` in the margin) plus your own words on separate slips. Don't highlight everything.
  - **Process**: (1) pull out all slips; (2) **cluster** them by your purpose, *orthogonal to the book's outline* (like an index), optionally drawing a mind map; (3) write Zettel per cluster: one general note, then detail notes, then feed the details back into the general note.
- **Principle of Atomicity** (as first stated here): "put things which belong together in a Zettel, but try to separate concerns." Keep assumptions and arguments in different notes, so arguments can be reused without the assumptions. (A 2025 update notes that "atomic notes" became a loaded term. Treat atomicity as a principle, not a rigid size rule.)
- **Functional margin glyphs** for dense theory texts: circle a defined term and write **D**; **M/μ** for a model; **A/α** for an argument; a lightning bolt for a weakness or disagreement.
- **Web/RSS triage**: **skim** feeds, never read them in full. Move keepers to a **reading list** (the inbox). Process the reading list like books: read, annotate, write notes. Put "collectibles" such as recipes on a reference list. Phones are good for filtering and poor for processing.
- **Citations**: put the full citation in every Zettel so each note stands alone. Use a reference manager (BibDesk, JabRef, Zotero) with citekeys such as `%a1%Y%t4` producing `mccracken2004bibd`, and text-expansion snippets for frequent works.

## Key Concepts
- **Barbell**: safe majority plus small, high-variance bets.
- **Intermediate notes**: paper slips of your own summaries, made while reading.
- **Cluster**: a purpose-driven group of reading notes, made before writing Zettel.
- **Orthogonal to the content**: clusters follow your intent, not the page order.
- **Reading list as inbox**: a commitment to attend to an item, not to keep it.
- **Citekey**: a stable ID for a source, used like a Zettel ID.
- **Integration bottleneck**: linking is the only step whose cost grows with archive size, a sign the archive is growing.

## Mental Models
- **Let the quality of the text set the time budget.** Your reading speed should not decide how much time a book gets.
- **Reading equals reading plus thinking plus writing.** Anything less is entertainment.
- **Ask what the text is for before you highlight.** Marks exist to help you understand later, not to help you remember now.
- **Go to primary sources.** Never rely on an author's own interpretation of the studies they cite.

## Anti-patterns
- **Speed reading as a goal**: lots of input, nothing processed.
- **Reading everything carefully**: wasted time on low-value books.
- **Marginal notes only**: your thoughts stay locked in the book, which is a Collector's Fallacy.
- **Rainbow highlighting**: low-depth processing.
- **Reading articles in full inside the RSS reader on mobile**: inefficient, because you will re-read to take notes, and ineffective, because no lasting note is made.
- **Converting slips one by one in page order**: you miss the book's architecture. Cluster first.

## Worked Example
Christian's processing of *Personal Kanban* (Benson & DeMaria Barry). After clustering the slips, he wrote notes from definitions up to synthesis (oldest first):

```
201307242017 Favor pulling tasks in Kanban                 (short, important detail)
201307242048 Concept Kaizen                                 (definition / tag anchor)
201307242052 Phases of (personal) Kanban
201307242104 Open loops are mental stress according to Zeigarnik
201307251804 Project-focussing Kanban for complex commitments
201307251930 Workflow of personal kanban
201307251958 Kanban in a nutshell                           (90-word synthesis, heavily linked)
```

He then connected the book to older notes, producing `Integrate Kanban into GTD via contexts` and `Agile project management with GTD, Kanban and reviews`. These are new ideas that exist in neither book.

What this shows: start with definitions and details, end with a dense hub note, then link sideways into the existing archive.

## Key Takeaways
1. Read once fast and mark. Read the marks again slowly and write.
2. Look for theories, models, definitions, arguments, counter-arguments, and facts.
3. Get ideas *out* of the book onto slips in your own words.
4. Cluster before writing Zettel, and organise by concept rather than chapter.
5. Separate concerns: assumptions, arguments, and definitions in different notes.
6. Every Zettel carries its full citation, and a reference manager holds the canonical data.
7. Skim streams, keep a reading list, and process it like books.
