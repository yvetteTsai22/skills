# Interview: English

Researched from English interviewing practice (oral history, journalism, customer interviews), not translated from the Chinese method. Sources at the end.

## How to ask

- **Past specifics over opinions.** Ask what happened, not what the author thinks would happen. "The last time this broke, what did you do?" beats "Do you think this matters?" One real incident outweighs many hypotheticals. (Mom Test)
- **Open questions, then "for example".** Start broad ("Tell me about the week you built it"), then pull the anecdote out: "Give me an example", "Walk me through that day", "Tell me more about that." (Journalist's Resource, Jerz)
- **Ask for the vivid detail.** "What do you remember seeing on the screen?" The first answer is usually general; the follow-up gives the line worth quoting. (Jerz)
- **Their style and language.** Let the author answer in their own words and define what matters; keep the recording-faithful version. (Oral History Association)
- **Objections through a third party.** "A skeptic would say X. What do you say?" gets a reply without a debate. (Journalist's Resource)
- **Plain terms.** When the answer is jargon, ask "Explain that to me in terms I'd understand." (Journalist's Resource, quoting Ron Suskind)
- **Suggested answer as a guess.** "My guess: X. Right?" gives the author something to correct; the correction carries the real detail.

## Question bank

| Slot | Example |
|---|---|
| Core claim | "If a reader remembers one sentence, which one?" |
| Timeline | "Walk me through it from the start. What were the stages, and what changed each time?" |
| Why the author | "What have you done here that most people writing about this haven't?" |
| Scene | "When did you first notice? Where were you, what was on the screen?" |
| Numbers | "How many, roughly? What was it before, and after?" |
| Objection | "A skeptic reads this. What's their first reply, and yours?" |
| Limits | "What doesn't work yet, or what are you unsure of?" |

## Sources (borrow tier, verified 2026-11-23)

- Oral History Association, *Best Practices*: open-ended questions, follow-ups for elaboration, narrators answer "in their own style and language". https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/ (official professional body)
- Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test*: ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future. http://momtestbook.com/ (well-known author; rule confirmed across independent summaries, e.g. https://readingraphics.com/book-summary-the-mom-test/)
- Journalist's Resource (Harvard Shorenstein Center), *Interviewing a source: Tips*: open prompts, re-ask in another way, "Your critics have said… What do you say?", Suskind's plain-terms request. https://journalistsresource.org/home/interviewing-a-source/
- Dennis Jerz, *Interviewing Tips for Journalists*: "Can you tell me a little more about that?", ask for vivid details, check facts after. https://jerz.setonhill.edu/writing/journalism1/interviewing-tips-for-journalists-before-during-and-after-you-talk-with-an-important-source-for-a-news-story/

The "ask for a specific past moment" rule appears in three independent sources above (Mom Test, Journalist's Resource, Jerz).
