# golden-line: English patterns

English golden lines come from concrete images and tight syntax, not from character play or rhyme. Chinese-only moves (1221 character inversion, splitting characters, rhyming couplets) do not carry over; use the figures below.

## The eight rules

1. Anchor every golden line in a concrete noun or a real detail from the draft.
2. Use not-X-but-Y only when X is something the reader really believes and the text has already said it.
3. Vary list length: two or four items. Use three only when the third item turns.
4. At most one one-line closer per section, and only after a long build-up.
5. Write lessons as conditionals ("If you X, Y"), not slogans.
6. Coin one or two named metaphors and reuse them through the piece.
7. Keep the highlight rate between 10% and 40%.
8. Open with a concrete claim, question or story. Close with a quiet inference, a question, or a line the piece has already set up and now repeats.

## Patterns

| Pattern | Move | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete metaphor | Map the abstract idea onto one physical object or scene; name it and reuse it (rule 6). | The draft explains a mechanism in abstract nouns |
| Specific detail | Replace the summary with the one number, name or object that proves it. | A sentence says "a lot", "often", "hard" |
| Conditional maxim | "If you X, Y." Put the condition first. | The draft states a lesson |
| Antithesis | Two balanced halves that oppose each other. | Two choices or two times are in tension |
| Chiasmus | Reverse the key words in the second half (A B / B A). Use once per piece at most. | A reversal is the point of the section |
| Callback | Repeat an earlier phrase at the end with new weight (epanalepsis at piece scale). | The opening set up a line worth paying off |

Checks before you keep a line:

- Is it true and as strong as the facts allow? Cut a hedge the facts do not need; add none they do. (Graham)
- Is the figure fresh? A metaphor you have seen in print is a cliché; replace it with one from the author's own material. (Orwell rule i)
- Can a word go? Cut it. Prefer the short word and the active verb. (Orwell rules ii–iv)
